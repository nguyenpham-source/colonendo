What was found, and what it means

For each finding: what the doctor saw through the scope, what the lab saw under the microscope, and what that means in plain words.

A · Polyp in the cecum

Harmless
Doctor saw
One flat-based (sessile) polyp, 5 mm, removed with a wire loop (cold snare). Removal complete.
Lab said
"Benign mucosal tag, negative for neoplasm or malignancy."

In plain words: it looked like a polyp, but under the microscope it was only a small flap of normal colon lining. It was not a growth and carries no cancer risk.

B · Two polyps in the ascending colon

Precancerous type · removed
Doctor saw
Two flat-based polyps, 3 to 5 mm, removed with a cold snare. Removal complete.
Lab said
"Subtle features of sessile serrated lesion without dysplasia."

In plain words:

  • Sessile serrated lesion (SSL) is a type of polyp with a saw-tooth pattern under the microscope. Left in place for many years, some SSLs can turn into cancer. That is why doctors remove them.
  • "Subtle features" means the pathologist saw only mild, early signs of this type.
  • "Without dysplasia" means the cells were not yet abnormal. This is the earliest, lowest-risk stage.
  • Both were completely removed, so they can't grow any further.

The lab received these as four small pieces (4–6 mm). Small polyps often come out in pieces with a snare; that is normal.

C · Diverticulosis throughout the colon

Common
Doctor saw
Multiple medium-mouthed diverticula in the entire colon.
Lab said
Nothing to test; this is seen by eye only.

In plain words: small pouches have formed where the colon wall is weaker. More than half of people over 60 have them. Most never have any problems. See the drawing and tips below.

Risk ladder

How far along were the polyps?

Polyps that can become cancer usually move through stages slowly, often over 10 years or more. Both findings were at the bottom of the ladder, and the risky ones were taken out.

Size

How small is 3 to 5 mm?

Doctors pay closer attention to polyps 10 mm (1 cm) or larger. All three of these were well under half that size.

Diverticulosis

The small pouches, explained

Day to day

  • Most people never notice them. No treatment is needed.
  • A high-fiber diet helps: vegetables, fruit, beans, oats, whole grains. A fiber supplement such as psyllium is an option.
  • Drink plenty of water and stay active.
  • Nuts, seeds and popcorn are generally fine. The old advice to avoid them is no longer recommended.

Call the doctor if you notice

  • Belly pain that doesn't go away, usually in the lower left, especially with fever, nausea or vomiting. This can be diverticulitis (an infected pouch).
  • Blood in the stool or from the rectum. A large amount, or dizziness, needs the emergency room.
